When I started out, "second shooter" wasn't even a phrase we used in Mysuru. You either had your own camera and your own clients, or you didn't shoot at all. If you were lucky, you assisted a senior photographer for a season, carried reflectors, changed lenses, and hoped someday you'd get a wedding of your own to call. There was no structure to it — no expectation that the person handing you the second camera owed you anything beyond a day's wage.

That's changed, and I think it needed to.

Over the last few years, as MDPVA has grown past 1,550 active members across seven taluk chapters, I've watched the second-shooter role shift from an informal favor into something closer to an apprenticeship — and honestly, into a real second income stream for a lot of our younger and part-time members. A second shooter today isn't just an extra pair of hands. They're covering candid moments the lead can't be in two places for, they're building their own portfolio shot by shot, and in many cases, they're a working photographer's first professional credit.

I want to talk about why building this culture deliberately — not leaving it to chance — matters for the district.

Why it matters

Mysuru's wedding and event season is dense. Studios that used to handle two or three bookings a weekend are now juggling five or six. That volume alone means senior members can't be everywhere. But more than capacity, second-shooting is how the next generation of photographers in this district actually learns the trade — not from a workshop slide, but standing next to someone during a live shoot, watching how they read a room, manage a difficult relative, or catch the exact second the garland exchange happens.

We've also seen it work the other way. Some of our most capable current members — people running their own studios today — started as second shooters for someone else in this very association. That's not an accident. That's the system working the way it should.

What we're doing about it

This is part of why membership includes access to the members-only job board and second-shooter calls — so that when a senior photographer needs backup for a big event, they're not scrambling through personal contacts, they're pulling from a verified, certified pool of MDPVA professionals. It's also why our workshops and photo walks matter beyond the technical skills they teach: they're where seniors and newer members actually meet each other, outside the pressure of a live shoot.

But a job board alone doesn't build a culture. Culture is built when senior members treat second shooters as colleagues-in-training rather than cheap labor, when credit is given where it's due, and when there's a shared understanding across the district that today's second shooter is tomorrow's lead. I'd ask our senior members to think of every second-shooter booking as an investment in the district's next generation of talent — because that's exactly what it is.

What I'd ask of newer members

If you're just starting out and second-shooting for someone, treat it as seriously as you would your own booking. Show up early. Ask questions before the event, not during it. And when you're ready to lead your own shoots, remember to bring someone up behind you the way someone (hopefully) brought you up.

Looking ahead

We're looking at ways to formalize this further — structured pairings for newer members through our chapters, and more visibility for second-shooter opportunities through the association. If you have thoughts on how we should shape this, our chapter meetings and the AGM are the right place to raise them.

Photography in this district has always been a community trade, passed hand to hand more than taught in a classroom. Building a real second-shooter culture is just us being honest about that — and making sure it works for everyone standing behind the camera, not just the one in front of the client.
