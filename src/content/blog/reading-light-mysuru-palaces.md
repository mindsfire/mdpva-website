I've photographed the Mysuru Palace more times than I can count — for clients, for the association, and plenty of times for no reason at all except that the light there never repeats itself the same way twice. If there's one thing I'd want every member of MDPVA to get comfortable with, it's this: the corridors of the Palace are one of the best places in this district to actually learn to read light, because the architecture forces you to.

Most of us learn light in open spaces first — gardens, lawns, the front of a mandapa. The Palace corridors don't give you that luxury. You're working with high stone arches, deep colonnades, painted ceilings, and light that enters only through specific openings at specific angles. If you can read a corridor like that correctly, reading light anywhere else becomes easier.

Morning corridors

Early morning, before the crowds build, the eastern-facing corridors get a low, raking light that comes almost parallel to the floor. This is when the pillar textures and the stone carving detail show best — the light grazes across the surface instead of flattening it. If you're shooting architectural detail or a couple wants formal portraits with that regal, textured backdrop, this is your window, and it's a narrow one. By mid-morning, the light steepens and that texture disappears into flat, even tone.

Midday — the hardest light to read

By noon, direct light barely reaches into the deeper parts of the corridors at all. What you're working with instead is bounced light — sunlight hitting the courtyard stone and reflecting back up into the shaded colonnade. It's soft, but it's directionless if you're not paying attention to where the reflection is actually coming from. I tell newer members: don't look at your subject first, look at the ground outside the corridor. That's your real light source at this hour.

Golden hour through stained glass and stone jaali

This is the one every photographer chases, and for good reason. As the sun drops, light coming through the carved stone jaali screens and the stained glass sections throws patterned shadows and color casts onto the corridor floor and walls that shift by the minute. It's dramatic, but it's also unpredictable — a pattern that looks perfect through your viewfinder can shift entirely in the ninety seconds it takes to reposition a couple for a portrait. My advice: scout the pattern first without your subject in frame, decide where you want them standing, and only then bring them in. Chasing the light with people already posed wastes the light.

Blue hour and interior lighting

Once the sun is down and the Palace's own lighting takes over, especially during illumination on Sundays and public holidays, you're dealing with mixed color temperature — warm tungsten from the Palace lights against a cooling blue sky. This is where white balance decisions matter more than gear. I'd rather members shoot this slightly on the warmer side and let the corridors keep their inviting, ceremonial character than correct it into something clinical.

A note on respect for the space

Beyond the technical side, I'd remind every member: the Palace is a heritage structure and an active place of daily visitors and worship-adjacent respect, not just a backdrop. Follow the photography permissions the authorities require, be mindful of tripod placement in narrow corridors during footfall hours, and never let a shoot block the path of visitors or staff.

If you're new to shooting there, I'd suggest spending a morning with no client at all — just walk the corridors slowly, watch how the light moves across a single pillar over twenty minutes, and you'll understand more about light than any workshop slide can teach you.
